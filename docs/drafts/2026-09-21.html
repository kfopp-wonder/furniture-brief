Good morning. Lovesac turned a year-ago loss into a profit on the back of $21 million in tariff refunds, proof that trade policy is now a line item on the income statement, not just a talking point. Canada answered U.S. tariffs with retaliatory duties of up to 50% on $20 billion in goods, adding fresh cost pressure just as builder confidence slid to its lowest mark in a year. Furniture Today's Sheila Long O'Mara asks the harder question underneath all of it: with consumers still spending elsewhere, is the industry's real rival simply the decision to wait. Add Wayfair's Monday Night Football debut and a wave of K-beauty at Target, and the throughline is clear: brands are fighting for attention and dollars that haven't disappeared, just gone elsewhere.

Top Stories

1. Lovesac Swings to Profit on $21 Million in Tariff Refunds

Lovesac (NASDAQ: LOVE) posted Q2 FY2027 net sales of $161.2 million, up 0.4% year over year, with gross profit up 21.7% to $110.3 million. Net income hit $7.4 million, or 51 cents per diluted share, reversing a $6.7 million loss a year ago, largely on $21 million in IEEPA tariff refunds. CEO Shawn Nelson credited high-end configuration and add-ons like Reclining Seat and Storage for resilience against a choppy category backdrop. Full-year guidance calls for sales of $690 million to $710 million, but Q3 guidance projects a net loss of $9 million to $12 million. Retailers watching Lovesac's margin story should note how much of the swing came from trade policy rather than demand, a distinction worth making with any vendor citing tariff relief as a pricing tailwind.

Source: Furniture Today

2. Canada Hits Back With Tariffs Up to 50% on U.S. Goods

Canada imposed retaliatory tariffs ranging from 15% to 50% on roughly $20 billion in U.S. goods, including steel, aluminum, household appliances and forestry products, after bilateral trade talks collapsed. The measures mirror the value of Canadian goods hit by U.S. tariffs last month and double Canada's duties on American steel and aluminum. Prime Minister Mark Carney framed the response as matching the U.S. dollar for dollar rather than raising revenue. For furniture, the direct exposure runs through steel, aluminum, forestry products and appliances, all inputs into case goods, hardware and delivery equipment. Sourcing teams should press suppliers now on whether Canadian-linked inputs carry new cost, before it shows up unannounced in Q4 invoices.

Source: Furniture Today

3. Builder Confidence Hits a One Year Low as Fed Hikes Rates

The NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index fell three points to 32 in September, its lowest level in a year, as the Fed raised interest rates for the first time since 2023. Thirty-eight percent of builders cut prices this month and 66% used sales incentives, both up from August. New home sales in July totaled 607,000, down 6.3% from a year earlier, and existing-home sales fell 1.7% in July to 4.06 million. Furniture sales track closely with home sales, since new floor plans and new bedrooms drive purchases. Retailers in markets with heavy new construction should expect a softer pipeline of move-in-driven big-ticket sales into year-end and lean harder on replacement and remodel messaging.

Source: Home News Now

4. Mattress Industry's Real Rival May Be Procrastination, Not the Economy

Furniture Today's Sheila Long O'Mara lays out the data behind the industry's favorite excuses: Consumer Confidence Index down to 89.4 in August, Michigan sentiment down 13.2% year over year, mattress unit shipments off 13.2% in 2025 per the International Sleep Products Association. But July retail and food service sales were still up 5% year over year, showing consumers are spending, just not on mattresses, since a worn-out one can always wait another year. The practical read for sales floors: a considered, postponable purchase needs a specific reason to happen now, not a wait for confidence or rates to improve. Retailers should arm associates with a concrete health-and-wellness case for buying today, not a general pitch about comfort.

Source: Furniture Today

5. Storefronts Need to Be Legible to AI Shopping Agents

New guidance argues retailers should structure product data and site architecture so AI shopping agents can find, understand and transact with a storefront, treating agentic traffic as a new channel rather than a threat. The excerpt frames this as a near-term retail imperative rather than a distant trend. For furniture and mattress retailers, that means clean, structured SKU data covering dimensions, materials, price and availability, the same discipline that underpins any endless-aisle or marketplace strategy. With online share of home furnishings estimated well into the 30% range depending on the source, an independent retailer whose product feed isn't machine-readable is invisible to a growing slice of shoppers before a human ever sees the listing.

Source: Humansecurity

Market Pulse

Retail & Consumer Trends

The move mirrors Ulta and Sephora both expanding K-beauty lines, signaling Korean brands' move from specialty retail into mass channels. For home furnishings, the lesson is about assortment refresh as a growth lever: Target is using a trending category to re-earn foot traffic and reset its financial trajectory, a strategy furniture retailers can apply with trending categories like wellness or outdoor.
    Source: Modern Retail

Her stated challenge now is avoiding distraction from growth and expanding physical retail only at a sustainable pace rather than chasing scale. For furniture DTC brands testing showrooms, the takeaway is the same discipline: floor samples paired with a controlled footprint beat rapid store expansion that outpaces fulfillment and cash flow.
    Source: Modern Retail
  • Beyond Credit Union Signals Retailers Chasing Financial Services - Neighborhood Intelligence, formerly Bed Bath & Beyond, is launching Beyond Credit Union with Alliant Credit Union in the first quarter of next year, covering checking, savings, credit cards and home equity products. CEO Marcus Lemonis pitched it as helping customers manage the cost of homeownership more intelligently. For furniture retailers already competing on financing terms for big-ticket purchases, a home-focused brand entering banking is a reminder that the credit relationship, not just the sale, is becoming a retention tool worth owning or partnering on.
    Source: Retail Dive

Supply Chain & Trade

  • Canada's $20 Billion Tariff Package Targets Steel, Appliances - Canada's new tariffs, effective at 12:01 a.m. Tuesday, range from 15% to 50% and cover steel, aluminum, household appliances, dairy and electronics, matching the value of U.S. duties imposed after talks collapsed Aug. 21. Economists cited by CBS flagged Midwestern manufacturers as especially exposed. Furniture Today notes the tariffs add to uncertainty around USMCA's future, a pact underpinning integrated North American supply chains. Sourcing and logistics teams should map any Canadian-linked components, from appliances to hardware, and confirm with vendors whether costs will be absorbed or passed through before Q4 orders lock in.
    Source: Furniture Today
  • Freight Costs, Not Just Tariffs, Are Squeezing Q4 Margins - Beyond tariffs, fallout from the Iran conflict is pushing ocean freight costs higher, with East Coast spot rates near $9,000 per 40-foot container in mid-August and West Coast rates up 11% week over week to around $6,800, per Furniture Today's Bill McLoughlin. Canada is the fourth-largest furniture exporter to the U.S. at $1.2 billion and the top buyer of U.S. furniture at $1.4 billion, making the trade dispute doubly disruptive. Haverty's CEO Steve Burdette cited a 25% to 30% jump in container rates from bunker fuel surcharges. Retailers should expect vendor price increases tied to freight, not just duties, heading into the fourth quarter and should ask suppliers to separate the two on any cost justification.
    Source: Furniture Today

The One Thing

Give Shoppers a Reason to Buy Now

Consumer sentiment is down and mattress unit shipments fell 13.2% in 2025, but retail sales are still up 5% year over year. The gap is procrastination, not empty wallets. This week, audit sales scripts and product data alike: make sure associates have a specific, health-based reason to buy today, and make sure that same product story is structured cleanly enough for online shoppers and AI agents to find it without a salesperson in the room.
